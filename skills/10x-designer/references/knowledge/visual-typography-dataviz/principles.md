# Visual, Typography & Data-Viz — Principles

Deduped, cross-referenced timeless principles.

## A. Foundations of layout & composition

- **The four base relationships: Contrast, Repetition, Alignment, Proximity (C.R.A.P.).** Every well-designed piece uses all four together; they are interconnected, never applied in isolation. Proximity = group related items so they read as one unit. Alignment = nothing placed arbitrarily; every element shares a visual edge/invisible line with another. Repetition = repeat a font/color/rule/shape/spacing to unify (consistency made conscious). Contrast = if two things differ, make them *very* different (near-sameness reads as a mistake).
- **Everything is connected — change one parameter, re-tune the rest.** Letter spacing, word spacing, line spacing, type size, and grid unit are interdependent; widen one and you must widen the others. Individual elements are noticeable only at the expense of the whole.
- **Use an underlying grid derived from a real constraint and a base unit, via ratios.** A grid begins from a fixed element (screen resolution, ad-unit size, image size, or type size) and a unit subdivided by rational (1:2, 2:3, 3:4) or irrational (Golden Section 1:1.618, DIN 1:1.414) ratios. It should be fine enough to be invisible yet flexible enough for varied content — the common denominator holding elements together, like bricks in a building.
- **Tie the grid unit to the type size for elastic harmony.** In print the unit is a multiple of the type size; on the web an em-based unit links typography and layout so the relationship survives user text-resizing.
- **Whitespace is active design material, not "empty space."** Macro whitespace (between blocks) and micro whitespace (between words/letters/lines) both do work: balance, brand positioning (more space = more luxury), and guiding the eye. Let it exist, keep it organized, and never trap it between elements.
- **Allocate space by importance — establish one focal point and a clear hierarchy.** The most significant information gets the most space and comes first (top-left in Western reading order); the least gets least. Without a single strong focal point the eye has no entry and nothing is emphasized; "if everything is large, nothing grabs attention." A reader should grasp focus, hierarchy, and flow at a glance.
- **Compose to lead the eye — Rule of Thirds, the triangle, and Looking Room.** Align focal points to thirds intersections (a practical proxy for the Golden Section); arrange ~3 focal points as a triangle for balance + movement; leave negative space in the direction a subject faces/moves. Centering bisects and deadens; off-center creates tension.
- **Adjacent elements that look identical get overlooked.** A row of identically sized/cropped/laid-out cards or promos reads as "the same thing." Vary size, crop, and placement (while keeping color/type cohesive) so each item is distinct and noticed.
- **Design a system (a kit of parts), not individual pages, and design to the worst case.** A grid + modular content objects + palette + type styles lets editors build any page. On the web the user controls content, so build for graceful degradation / progressive enhancement (200% text, JS off, wrong-size image, old browser).

## B. Typography

- **Type is a voice — every choice communicates before a word is read.** Within a split second the arrangement and look of type creates an impression, as we react to a person's presence before knowing them. Even a "neutral" face signals neutrality. Match the typeface tone to the message's emotion and brand (purpose, not fashion or taste).
- **For body text the design should be invisible; we read best what we read most.** Familiar, self-effacing letterforms (oldstyle romans — Garamond, Caslon, Baskerville) are perceived as most legible largely through cultural exposure. Hardworking text faces "look so normal you don't notice you're reading them."
- **We read by word shapes, not letter by letter — so never set running text in ALL CAPS.** The eye scans the tops of x-heights and the outline of ascenders/descenders to assemble words. All caps are uniform rectangles read letter-by-letter, slowing reading sharply.
- **Type sizes should follow a related modular scale, not arbitrary values.** Centuries-old scales work because each size relates to the others by ratio; "sizes from the dropdown" clash like discordant music. Derive the whole hierarchy from one base via a consistent ratio.
- **Establish hierarchy with a consistent weight/style pecking order, and more levels for more complex content.** Roman is body; emphasis moves predictably to bold/italic/small-caps/caps (never reversed). Linear reading (a novel) needs one face at one size; catalog/manual/editorial content needs several deliberate, consistent levels (size, weight, color, alternate face) so browsers and scanners can enter anywhere. Don't change levels randomly page to page.
- **Constrain line length (the measure) — ~45–75 characters; never past ~90.** There is an optimum width; too wide and the eye can't find the next line, too narrow and rhythm breaks. (Working range roughly ~52–78; other authors place it ~45–75.)
- **Leading (line spacing) must be wider than word spacing — and spacing scales with line length.** When leading exceeds word space the eye moves *along* the line, not *down* between lines. As lines lengthen, widen word space, line space, and tracking together.
- **Reading distance/speed dictates layout (marathon vs. sprint).** Long-distance reading wants comfortable settled rhythm (longer lines, generous spacing); short-burst scanning (ads) wants short, compact, tighter lines. Match line length, spacing, and typeface verve to consumption mode.
- **Combine typefaces by contrast, never by conflict — and never two faces from the same category.** Concord = one family (calm). Conflict = two similar-but-not-same faces (reads as a mistake; avoid). Contrast = clearly distinct faces (intentional). To diagnose a bad pairing, name the *similarities*. Pull combined faces from different categories and reinforce the difference across multiple axes (Size, Weight, Structure, Form, Direction, Color) — structure alone (serif vs. sans) is rarely enough.
- **Trust your eyes, not nominal point size.** Point size is a metal-body convention; two 20-point faces can look wildly different because x-height, width, and contrast vary. What you see is what you get.
- **Use the correct glyph for the job.** True curly quotes/apostrophes (not straight inch/foot marks); en dash (–) for ranges, em dash (—) for breaks, hyphen (-) to join; one ellipsis character; one space after a period; em-space (≈ type size) for indents — and use indent OR space-between-paragraphs, never both; italics not underline for emphasis; real bullets not hyphens.
- **Proportional fonts beat monospaced for reading.** Monospace stretches the i and cramps the m; reserve it for typewriter imitation, plain email, or code.
- **Type for the medium and conditions.** Use faces engineered for screen (hinting, reduced contrast, robust weight — Georgia, Verdana, Source/Fira Sans) below ~14px; use sturdy weights that won't drop out or fill in at low resolution; use larger high-contrast type (black on yellow) for instructions read in bad conditions; disambiguate confusable glyphs (1/l/I, 0/O, per DIN 1450) in numerals, forms, signage, and code.

## C. Color

- **Design in greyscale/tone first; add color last.** Resolve composition, contrast, and structure in black-white-grey so the design doesn't *rely* on color to function (also aids accessibility and colour-blind users).
- **A palette is a relationship, not a list — assign Dominant, Subordinate, Accent roles.** One dominant color carries the message, a subordinate supports/contrasts, an accent draws the eye. All-competing palettes have no focus and no story.
- **Color meaning is cultural — choose for the audience, not your taste.** Red = anger/heat (West) vs. wealth/fortune (East); white = purity (West) vs. mourning (East). Getting it wrong can be disastrous.
- **Warm colors advance, cool colors recede — don't force equal weight; vary tones for harmony.** Use less of the hot color, more of the cool. Equal-area warm/cool fights; similar-toned hues look muddy (harmony needs tonal variety).
- **Use the correct color model for the medium.** RGB (additive, screen, 72 ppi) vs. CMYK (subtractive, print, 300 dpi). Mismatched models lose data and brilliance.
- **For data-encoding color, use perceptually calibrated palettes (ColorBrewer).** Default categorical colors are fine for prototyping; real sequential/categorical encoding needs palettes engineered for perceptual uniformity and discriminability.

## D. Data visualization & integrity (the canon)

- **A graphic must tell the truth — visual representation must match the numbers (graphical integrity).** The physically measured size of every element should be directly proportional to the quantity it represents; the eye's perceived effect must match the data's effect. This is the basis of the **Lie Factor** = (size of effect shown) ÷ (size of effect in data); 1.0 is honest, outside ~0.95–1.05 is distortion.
- **Encode quantitative magnitude by length/position — never map a 1-D value to 2-D area or 3-D volume, and never scale by radius.** The eye reads total area/ink, not a linear dimension; area grows as the square and volume as the cube, so radius- or area-scaling wildly exaggerates. For sized shapes, compute radius from area: r = √(area/π).
- **Show data variation, not design variation.** Changes in the graphic should reflect changes in the data, not arbitrary scale, baseline, zoom, perspective, or decoration. Bar charts must use a zero baseline. Deflate/standardize monetary time series (nominal units pass inflation off as real growth). Hold scales constant across panels.
- **Context is essential — never quote data out of context; "compared with what?"** Show enough surrounding data (more time points, adjacent comparison groups) so the eye can judge whether a change is real. A truncated window manufactures or hides effects.
- **Strip decoration — show the data, keep attention on substance.** The most ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space. Non-data ink (chartjunk, ornament, gratuitous 3-D/perspective, excess ticks/gridlines) competes with and can reverse the data's reading; if the design draws attention to itself it has failed.
- **Plot the data; graphics reveal structure that summary statistics hide.** Four datasets can share an identical mean, variance, regression line, and correlation coefficient yet look utterly different when plotted. Excellence is nearly always multivariate — show as many relevant dimensions as the data carry (a classic example is a six-variable map of a military campaign).
- **The chart type is determined by the data relationship, not aesthetics.** Identify the relationship first — nominal comparison, time series, ranking, part-to-whole, deviation, distribution, correlation — then pick from the valid chart types. Some per-type rules are non-negotiable (pies sum to 100% and stay ≥5–7 slices; time on the x-axis left→right; ≤4 lines or use small multiples).
- **Visualization is a deliberate mapping of data to visual properties — and you should build it on scales, not raw pixels.** You author the rules that express values as visual properties. Drive position/size through a scale (domain→range) with a dynamic domain so the chart stays correct as data changes; keep the scale (math) separate from the axis (its visual representation). Pad the plot so nothing clips; label ticks as clean round numbers.
- **Information first, design second; form follows function.** The information is the most important part; design applies a visual solution to a communication problem. If the data is incomplete, untrustworthy, or uninteresting, no design saves it.
- **Sources determine credibility: few, reliable, recent, complementary.** Prefer one source (two or three acceptable); the more sources the more variance/bias. Use unbiased producers, data ≥1 year old (2 if unavoidable), and always disclose the data's age.

## E. Purpose, audience & communication

- **Define the communication objective before designing — it dictates the approach, not your taste.** Order the three provisions (Appeal / Comprehension / Retention) by goal: analytical work → Comprehension first; marketing → Appeal first; editorial → Appeal then Comprehension. The same data set is designed differently for different goals.
- **Distinguish exploratory from explanatory (narrative) and design for one.** Exploratory views present data neutrally for discovery across many quick looks (best for science); explanatory/narrative views are focused, communicating only the points already found (best for editorial/marketing). For interactive views, follow the standard mantra: **overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand.** A chart trying to be both does neither well.
- **Leverage preattentive attributes for instant comprehension.** Color, size, orientation, position, and shape are perceived in ~250 ms before conscious attention; encode the key signal in one so viewers detect patterns, trends, and outliers almost instantly. Images suit spatial structure/detail; words suit logic, procedure, and abstraction — the strongest pieces pair both.
- **Show, don't tell; combine words and images.** Illustrate the subject rather than describing it; a picture plus a facial expression carries richer meaning than either alone. Use illustration in a supporting role only — once it overrides or distorts the data it is no longer an infographic; tasteful decoration aids long-term retention but not at the cost of integrity.
- **Abstraction creates connection; match fidelity to audience.** Reducing detail makes a figure "nobody and everybody," letting viewers project onto it; over-realism distracts and triggers the Uncanny Valley. Rough for peers, refined for execs/customers.
- **Constraints force clarity; be direct.** Limiting to 3-8 panels (or 5 seconds, or 140 characters) forces you to find the salient point and cut the rest. Use active voice, combine related points, write in the user's authentic voice (contrived hype is detected). A landing page must convey "what is this / why care" in ~5 seconds.
- **Sequence matters — guarantee readability between steps; establish context before detail.** In any multi-step flow, a reader must infer what happened between two adjacent steps (meaning lives in the "gutter"); keep continuity cues consistent. Open with context ("forest first, then trees"). Vary the shot to avoid monotony, but never so much that orientation breaks.
- **Naming a problem gives you power over it.** You cannot fix what you cannot name; verbalize *why* a piece works or fails.
- **Learn the rules before you break them — and break them only deliberately.**
