# Principles — Behavior, Emotion & Persuasion

Deduplicated, timeless principles. Numbers and named laws preserved.

## 1. Emotion & the irrational user

- **P1. All design is emotional — you cannot NOT design for emotion.** Every artifact, word, color, and empty state sends an emotional signal and is assigned a personality whether you intend it or not. Emotion dominates decision-making, commands attention, and tags memory; people whose emotional centers are damaged cannot make even trivial decisions. There is no emotionally neutral detail.

- **P2. Design for the user who actually exists — tired, busy, biased, on autopilot — not the rational decision-maker.** ~95% of cognition is nonconscious; millions of bits per day enter the brain. Decisions are gut/emotion first, reason second; emotion is the tie-breaker among equally valid options. Designing for an idealized rational user makes your design, at best, as good as chance. To get action, make the benefit *feel* greater than the cost — don't build an exhaustive rational case.

- **P3. Emotionally charged experiences burn into long-term memory, and negatives outweigh positives roughly 2× (Law of Hedonic Asymmetry).** The amygdala tags emotional events "remember this." Negative experiences demand more attention, are remembered better, and skew judgment — so **fix problems (remove the negative) before adding features (add the positive).** Total *remembered* experience matters more than momentary perfection; invest in the emotional arc, not edge-case flawlessness.

- **P4. Emotion is renewed on every interaction; design for the use lifetime, not the conversion moment.** Purchase-decision emotions (cost anxiety, bargain delight) fade and are overwritten by the emotions felt while *using* the product over its lifetime. Optimize the ongoing experience, not just the funnel.

## 2. Authentic emotion vs. bolted-on persuasion

- **P5. Build "supported" emotion into the artifact; don't rely on "associated" emotion from marketing.** Supported emotions (delivered by the product's own form, features, interaction, copy) are authentic, experienced every use, cheap, and durable. Associated emotions (created by advertising and linked by repetition) are superficial, costly to sustain, and collapse when the product underdelivers.

- **P6. Every emotional claim must be backed by the experience — authenticity is non-negotiable.** A "feeling of premium/secure/fast/caring" must tie to actual premium/security/speed/care. Claims the product doesn't deliver flip positive emotion into anger and betrayal (marketing that promises more than the interaction delivers always backfires). Contrived or gimmicky personality trips the brain's "bad-for-me" detector and destroys trust.

- **P7. Emotion/quality in design is usually cheap — it's the cost of thought, not material.** Thoughtful color, form, wording, and interaction typically cost the same to produce as drab alternatives — appealing packaging or a well-chosen brand color can add far more perceived value than its marginal cost. When someone says "we can't afford to polish this,\" the polish is usually near-free.

## 3. Aesthetics is functional

- **P8. Aesthetic-usability effect: attractive things are perceived as — and effectively are — more usable, more credible, and more persuasive.** Beauty puts people in a positive mood that improves creative problem-solving, so they find solutions more easily and forgive friction. "All else equal, attractive things are more persuasive than unattractive ones." Visual polish also signals behind-the-scenes care, which is what builds trust in money/data-sensitive products. Aesthetics is not frosting.

- **P9. Reason about emotion on two axes and three layers.** Axes: **value** (pleasant↔unpleasant → approach vs. avoid → Friendly↔Unfriendly personality) and **arousal** (low↔high stimulation → motivation/attention/memory intensity → Dominant↔Submissive personality). Layers: **Visceral** (aesthetics, judged first/unconsciously) → **Behavioral** (interaction/usability; key requirement = feedback) → **Reflective** (meaning/identity/trust over time). Design for the unconscious response: it is consistent, stable, and far easier to predict than conscious appraisal.

- **P10. Match arousal/richness to context of use.** Experiential/entertainment products (low task challenge) should be aesthetically richer and more arousing; task-oriented and high-stress products (medical, finance, error-recovery) should be calmer and less rich, because **stress narrows attention to tunnel vision and kills creative problem-solving** (Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U). Saturation and figure-ground contrast drive arousal/attention largely independent of hue; hue *meaning* is culturally conditioned and must be localized.

## 4. Personality, relationship & trust

- **P11. Give the product one clear, authentic personality and keep it consistent across every channel.** People connect with people, not machines. Express ONE coherent personality across visuals, microcopy, voice/tone, error states, packaging, and marketing; conflicting signals read as dishonesty and erode trust ("a great brand means one thing only: trust"). Document it (design persona: traits, voice, personality map of friendly↔unfriendly × submissive↔dominant, visual lexicon). A clearly expressed, even polarizing personality beats a bland neutral one — differentiation filters out wrong-fit users and an emotional reaction beats indifference.

- **P12. Relationships form in sequence: Attract → Converse → Transact (trust before transaction).** Attract via aesthetics (unconscious); Converse via usable interaction that follows human social rules; Transact only after trust is earned. Mirrors the funnel **Awareness → Knowledge → Liking → Trust** (visitors judge whether to trust a site in under one second). Skipping or failing an earlier stage blocks the later ones.

- **P13. Products follow human social rules: respond, acknowledge, defer.** Users apply etiquette to interfaces as readily as they would to a person. Give immediate feedback to every action (non-response reads as a status snub / broken conversation); greet on arrival and acknowledge transitions; obey Quality, Quantity, Relevance, Clarity in copy. We are **attracted to the similar but prefer to interact with the complementary** — for assistant/tool roles the product should defer (submissive) and let the user lead; flattery of novices is believed and preferred over silence.

## 5. Focus, friction & the funnel

- **P14. Exactly one primary goal per page/screen; everything else supports or is cut.** Multiple competing CTAs of equal visual weight split attention and lower conversion; the action competes with every other element for scarce attention. A page may carry a secondary micro-conversion, but the primary action gets its own space and clear visual dominance. Tie every feature/element to the goal (and to the stated emotion/value strategy); cut what doesn't serve it.

- **P15. Reduce friction and FUDs at every step — but add deliberate friction at high-stakes moments.** Most of the time, minimize the user's work: strip forms to essentials, default everything possible, scan-don't-read (apply the two-second rule), put the user's desired outcome on the path of least resistance. FUDs (**F**ears = security/privacy, **U**ncertainties = usability/navigation/errors, **D**oubts = product/company) create funnel holes and escalate toward checkout. **BUT** friction is also a *tool*: at irreversible, consequential, or harm-prone actions (purchases, posting, returns) add purposeful friction — confirmation prompts, slow content, reflective speed bumps — so users engage deliberate thinking and make fewer regret-prone errors. Frictionless is not always ethical.

- **P16. The single most powerful move is to engineer the action away ("Cheat").** Remove the user's work entirely — default it (auto-enrollment in retirement plans roughly doubles participation), make it incidental to something they already do (iodized salt), or automate the repetition — so the user need only give informed consent. There are no martyrs in beneficial behavior change; difficulty is not a virtue.

- **P17. All five behavior-change prerequisites must coincide in time; more motivation/ability has diminishing returns.** **C**ue → **R**eaction (fast thinking) → **E**valuation (slow thinking) → **A**ability → **T**iming → Execute. A potential action leaks at every stage and each stage is *relative* — the action must out-compete what the user is already doing. Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Trigger; when an action is already easy or already motivating, adding more of either changes little; the big wins come from the missing **cue** and from removing a hard **barrier**. Don't over-invest in persuasion when the gap is a missing trigger.

- **P18. Match information depth and page structure to buying stage and temperament.** Buying stages: Need recognition → Information search → Evaluation → Purchase → Post-purchase; only a small fraction of visitors are in the purchase stage at any given moment. Address all four temperaments on one page in order: **Aggressive** (strong headline + unique value, first) → **Impulsive** (clear CTA + scannable benefits, second) → **Caring** (testimonials/social proof, third) → **Logical** (full detail, last). Design from a handful of representative personas, not "the masses."

## 6. Habits, motivation & measurable outcomes

- **P19. Build new habits; never fight existing ones head-on.** Habits are automatic and never truly un-form (they survive brain damage, surgery, dementia). To change one, avoid the cue, replace the routine (keep cue+reward), or crowd it out — don't rely on willpower. To *build* one, use an unambiguous single-purpose cue, an unvarying routine, and an **immediate** meaningful reward (so dopamine reinforces the link before memory fades).

- **P20. Define success as a concrete, measurable real-world OUTCOME — never a state of mind; separate outcome/actor/action.** "Users understand nutrition" is unmeasurable and probably not the real goal; "average BMI of 24" is. Equating the action with the outcome hides the risky assumption that the action causes the outcome; target the behavior that *directly* causes it and ship the **minimum viable action**. Beware **Goodhart's Law**: when an easy-to-measure metric (clicks, comments, features shipped) becomes the target it gets gamed and detaches from value.

- **P21. Reward and incentive design: protect intrinsic motivation; vary rewards to sustain engagement.** Direct cash payments for a behavior the user already had intrinsic reason to do crowd out intrinsic/social motivation and the behavior collapses when payment stops. Predictable rewards flatten engagement; **variable rewards** (varying greetings, surprises, deals) sustain anticipation — but variable/partial reinforcement is also the most *addictive* schedule (slot machines, loot boxes), so it is ethically loaded.

## 7. Wholeness, the journey & the unseen

- **P22. Design the whole journey home-to-home, and design the unhappy path as rigorously as the happy path.** The experience starts before the user reaches the product and continues after they leave; peripheral/in-between surfaces (loading, waiting, confirmation, 404s, empty states) are high-leverage because expectations there are low. Optimize the *whole funnel*, not one page (fixing a landing page while checkout leaks most of it accomplishes nothing). And by **survivorship bias** (analyses that only study survivors miss where the real failures happened), run a red-team / speculative worst-case exercise to surface harms and failure modes the optimistic team can't see.

- **P23. Manage the *perception* of waiting, not just the duration.** How a wait is handled matters more to satisfaction than its raw length. Announce expected time up front, slightly over-quote (a shorter-than-quoted wait beats a longer one), and fill the wait with engaging/informative content. Always give immediate feedback / a progress indicator for any action longer than a few seconds.

- **P24. Hide the machinery (separate onstage from backstage) — but never hide the truth.** Screen internal machinery, raw errors, and seams behind clean status/empty/error states. This is *not* a license to obscure cost, terms, or material change: hiding the math, burying opt-outs, or dense off-path legal copy is deliberate obfuscation. Honest "backstage" hides clutter; dark "backstage" hides things the user would object to.

- **P25. A single out-of-place defect can negate the whole experience — and the experience decays without maintenance.** The themed/branded experience is fragile: one typo, dead link, broken image, stale data, or mismatched element breaks the illusion and credibility. Even a flawless design degrades after launch (neglect spreads; returning users arrive with higher expectations). Build maintenance and continuous polishing into the product.

## 8. Ethics & power (the dark-pattern firewall)

- **P26. Disclosure test: if you'd be ashamed to tell the user *why* you designed it this way, it's a dark (or gray) pattern.** Every persuasive technique — contrast/decoy pricing, defaults, anchoring, loss-aversion framing, scarcity — has a good and an evil use; the only difference is whose interest it serves and whether the user would endorse it on reflection. This is the master arbiter.

- **P27. Three bright lines you must not cross: involuntariness, concealment, and emotional manipulation into poor choices.** Voluntary change requires conscious choice and informed consent. Specifically: don't take actions on the user's behalf without disclosure/opt-out; tell users what you're doing; never manipulate emotions so people make choices against their interest. Ask whether a stranger would trust it.

- **P28. Concrete dark-pattern prohibitions** (each ethical when inverted to the user's favor):
  - **Opt-out is presumed consent.** Make consent/marketing/data/add-on checkboxes **opt-in** (unchecked).
  - **Symmetric effort.** Cancellation/unsubscribe/deletion must be at least as easy as sign-up.
  - **Honest anchors and scarcity.** No fake "was $X" prices, fabricated countdowns, or false "N viewing/low stock."
  - **Show the math; show real money.** Disclose all-in price and fees before the user invests effort/data; show line-item savings; show real-currency value behind tokens/credits (breakage profits from unspent balances).
  - **Genuine social proof.** Trust seals/testimonials must be third-party verifiable and FTC-compliant — self-issued seals correlate with *lower* trustworthiness.

- **P29. What you leave OUT, and who is at the table, are design decisions.** Concealing biasing-but-true information (name, photo, school, employer, race on evaluative screens) produces fairer decisions — adding fields is the designer's reflex, but sometimes less information yields better outcomes. And if you ignore power, you scale existing inequities: bring the most-impacted, least-powerful users into the decisions that affect them.

- **P30. Framing determines the conversation; align stakeholders to one higher-order purpose.** Ask "How might we…\" not "Should we…" to open the solution space instead of a polarized fight. People optimize what they're measured/compensated on, not the true goal, so a shared purpose statement becomes the arbiter cutting through siloed self-interest (a ranked set of values that pre-decides tradeoffs works better than an unordered list). To get sign-off on change from risk-averse stakeholders, frame it as a **loss avoided** (losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please), and counter decision fatigue by scheduling the riskiest decision first.

## 9. Inclusion & change management

- **P31. Accommodate limitations without stigma; match real needs, not stereotypes.** Assistive/accessibility features that visibly mark the user as needing help get rejected. Likewise, "shrink it and pink it" addresses a superficial stereotype, not real needs; identity inputs as fixed radio sets (male/female/Other) erase identities and visually privilege the first option (notational bias) — use open fields that match the real mental model.

- **P32. Prefer "you may" over "you must" when changing the interface; rewarded goodwill buys forgiveness.** Forcing change provokes backlash; foreshadow, roll out gradually (velvet-rope), and offer an opt-out, because familiarity itself is pleasurable and even strict improvements unsettle users. Accrued positive emotion acts as insurance: rewarded users forgive outages, bugs, and mistakes — but during a failure, lead with honest facts and reassurance *before* any levity.
