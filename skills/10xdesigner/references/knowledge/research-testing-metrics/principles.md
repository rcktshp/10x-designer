# Research, Testing & Metrics — Principles

Deduplicated, cross-referenced principles from 12 books on research, testing, and metrics.

---

## Method selection & research stance

- **Quantitative tells you WHAT; qualitative tells you WHY — always triangulate.** Numbers/analytics/A-B locate where and how much; only talking to and watching people reveals motivation, intent, and cause. A surprising, flat, or ambiguous number is a prompt to go qualitative, not to ship.
- **Match the method to the question, not to fashion.** Generative ("what's up with…?"), descriptive ("what & how?"), evaluative ("are we getting close?"), causal ("why?") each demand different methods; neither qual nor quant is universally superior. Interviews are for depth/context (not pricing, future prediction, or pure prototype reactions); usability testing for prototype reactions; analytics for scale; A/B for isolated causal effects.
- **Generative research must drive IA, navigation, and interaction; evaluative/preference research cannot.** Preference data (surveys, focus groups, mood boards) suits branding; evaluative data (usability, card sort, analytics) fixes interaction/layout/labels; only generative research (non-directed interviews, ethnography, diaries, mental models) explores the mind-space before ideation. Using preference data to build a product is "building a kitchen from magazine clippings."
- **Behavior beats opinion — observe what people do, don't just ask.** Focus groups and surveys report what people feel like telling you (and how they wish to be seen), which differs from what they do. "Everybody lies"; people are poor reporters and predictors of their own behavior. Self-reported success ≠ actual success (a user "buys" a ticket he never bought). Use self-report only to illuminate behavior.
- **You are not your user; design for the audience, not yourself.** "Next-bench" / self-design bakes the team's age, ability, skill, jargon, and habits into the product as accidental barriers. Even enthusiast-founders are too caught up being the user. The Segway failed by ignoring transportation context. Run every decision through ≥3 other behavioral perspectives; trace each task end-to-end from a user who lacks the maker's context.
- **Cheap tests first, expensive tests later; test early, test often.** Sketches/paper before prototypes; office before field; general before hard-to-find audiences; a competitor before you put pencil to paper. One test beats none ("testing with one user is 100% better than none"). The worst practice is testing only at the end before launch (huge problems, no slack); the most expensive testing is what your customers do for you after launch.
- **Reality always wins — cultivate a desire to be proven wrong cheaply.** You can never prove a hypothesis, only disprove it (Popper's black swan). Say "we predict," not "we believe." Being disproven early and cheaply is the most valuable learning; a "failed" test that taught you something is a success.
- **Don't lead the user.** Task prompts, screener questions, and moderator scripts must be neutral. Supplying the exact on-screen term/spelling turns findability into a paste/Ctrl-F game and fakes success ("pediatric cerebrovascular"; "Customize your LAUNCHcast station"). Ask only open who/what/when/where/why/how; introduce no vocabulary the participant hasn't used; never present a concept as "something I've been working on."
## Hypotheses, goals & metrics

- **Define success criteria, error definitions, and the metric of interest BEFORE collecting data.** A task needs a clear end state ("find IBM's 5-year gain," not "research retirement"). Fix the metric, stopping rule, α, power, and test direction up front. Choosing the metric or one-tailed test after seeing results capitalizes on chance and inflates your true error rate ("never flout a convention just once").
- **A clear, falsifiable hypothesis is the north star.** Form: "For [user group], if [change] then [effect] because [rationale], which will impact [measure]." Agree up front what you'll learn if it SUCCEEDS and if it FAILS. Design to learn, not only to ship.
- **The best metrics align user needs with business goals — and must be falsifiable.** Question any metric guaranteed to improve (a feature's success measured by clicks on that feature "can't fail"). Tie metrics to a downstream business behavior (retention, conversion, completion) or they aren't actionable. Derive goals top-down: org purpose → site's role → desired user action → measurable signal — then check users actually *want* to do it.
- **No single number is meaningful in isolation — context is mandatory.** Judge any metric only against (a) comparable pages/users, (b) the same metric over time, (c) its proportion of a total. Eye-tracking and analytics metrics have no useful external/industry benchmarks. Compare ≥2 designs/versions/competitors/AOIs; compare pages only within the same page type/template.
- **Prefer rates/proportions over raw counts.** Counts move with traffic and seasonality and mislead; reframe success as a rate with a meaningful denominator (conversion rate, bounce rate, % exit).
- **Measure performance AND satisfaction — they don't always correlate.** Users struggle yet rate highly, or breeze through yet rate poorly (task-time/rating correlations ranged −0.41 to +0.06 across 10 studies). Never judge on a single metric class. Faster task time is almost always better from the *user's* perspective — reject owner-centric "stickiness/longer session" framing (except games/e-learning).
- **A UX metric must be observable, quantifiable, and tied to actual use** — not abstract preference. Set baseline and target metrics even for brand-new products; "no comparison exists" is no excuse to skip them.
- **Performance/behavioral metrics tell you WHAT happened, never WHY.** Task success, time, and analytics pinpoint problem tasks/steps but never their causes; supplement with observation, verbatims, or interviews before shipping a fix.
- **Distinguish statistical from practical significance.** A p-value only says a difference is unlikely to be chance; the confidence interval around the *difference* (effect size) tells you whether users would notice. With large samples (n>100, A/B tests), trivial differences become "significant."
- **Always report a confidence interval, not just a point estimate.** Every sample statistic is off by some amount; small UX samples (5–20) make bare point estimates untrustworthy. Non-overlapping error bars ⇒ significant; wide overlap ⇒ not; slight overlap ⇒ run a test.
- **You cannot predict how much a design change will move a metric.** Too many interacting variables (size, color, copy, placement, device, motivation). State *which* metric and direction you expect; never promise "+5%." Small change ≠ small impact and global redesign ≠ big impact — "global vs. local" describes how different the experience is, not the size of the movement (a 5px LinkedIn header change dropped ad CTR).
- **Data resolves debates without rank — believe the data, not the HiPPO.** (Highest Paid Person's Opinion.) Data literacy across roles makes the customer the decision-maker — less risky than gut feeling. Counterintuitive results are common; the mental model is the neutral artifact that referees feature/department politics.
- **Always answer "Why?" before "How?" — optimization only reaches a local maximum.** Split testing climbs the current hill; it can't tell you a different design reaches a far higher peak. "Yahoo could A/B test forever and never become Google." Know the concept/strategy before tuning metrics.
## Mental models, intent & empathy

- **"Intuitive" means "matches the user's mental model."** The closer the interface fits the user's pre-existing internal model, the easier to learn and use; novelty offers no hooks and is often unusable. Mental models change slowly (over decades), so durable strategy can be built on them — derive IA from the user's mental spaces, not the org chart, and it lasts ~10 years instead of being re-architected every 2.
- **Design to what people are trying to accomplish (intent/purpose), not to what your product does.** Pursue the "story of why" (saving for an archaeological dig, not "saving money"). Scope research around the person's larger purpose, broader than your product. Use the customer's own verbs and vocabulary; capture root intent at "Hallway Test" granularity (tool-agnostic, neither mechanics nor vague goals).
- **Empathy is knowledge developed before it is applied — and reasoning is evergreen.** Build cognitive empathy by listening to a person's reasoning, reactions, and guiding principles; only then "try on" their perspective. Reasoning patterns don't change with tools, so this knowledge stays valid for a career; tool-specific feedback decays. Leaping to "I know what users want" produces assumption-driven failure (BlackBerry, Windows 8, Netflix Qwikster).
- **Personas/segments are behavioral design targets, not demographic/marketing groups.** Segment by what people do (a "rotating cast" varying per scene), not by age/gender/income (correlation ≠ causation). Design targets ≠ marketing targets; designing for less expertise also serves experts. Name segments after performers/roles, not actions. Solve for 2–3 reasoning patterns, not one generalized happy path.
- **Every feature must map to a real user behavior and have exactly one primary home.** Orphan features (the "stock ticker on the intranet") serve internal/political ends; remove or verify. Multiple equally-valid primary homes signal an under-defined/compound feature. Towers with only secondary support are unmet-need gaps — the highest-value opportunities.
- **Usability is the absence of frustration — and it's necessary but not sufficient.** A product is usable when users do what they want, the way they expect, without hindrance/hesitation/questions; you can only measure how *un*usable it is (count problems, what and why). Passing usability clears the minimum bar — it won't give you vision or market success.
- **Usability has measurable attributes; usefulness gates them all.** Useful (meets the actual goal — most often overlooked), efficient, effective, learnable, satisfying, accessible. If users value the benefits they tolerate more problems; if not, perfect usability is moot. Tie goals to measurable, operational criteria.
## Sampling, rigor & study quality

- **Representativeness beats randomness; the wrong population can't be fixed by any statistic.** "Better decisions come from a less-than-perfectly-random sample of the right population than a perfect sample of the wrong one." Draw one sample then randomly assign to test/control — never compare test to "everybody else" or by arrival order/time-of-day.
- **Formative testing finds problems; summative testing measures — don't conflate them.** Formative = small-sample, iterative, find-and-fix, flexible protocol, no statistics needed; summative = benchmark/comparative, metric-driven, fixed protocol, larger samples. "The point is to *improve* the design, not document weaknesses."
- **Iterate in small rounds across changed conditions, not one big study.** Spend a 15-user budget as three rounds of 5 (or "a morning a month" of 3 users), fixing the worst problems between rounds — "fishing in multiple ponds." More rounds catch more problems over time and keep the team engaged.
- **The "5 users find 85%" rule is conditional, not a law.** It holds only when problem-discovery rate p≈0.31; websites often have p≈0.04, where 5 users find ~14%. Size formative studies from n = ln(1−goal)/ln(1−p). For qualitative find-and-fix the real question is "as many as you can fix before next round" — a common recommendation is batches of 3. The "30 participants for eye tracking" rule is a debunked myth.
- **Wide task coverage beats more participants for finding issues.** CUE-4: no significant correlation between participant count and % problems found, but strong correlation with number of tasks (r=0.82). Allocate a fixed budget toward more tasks.
- **Beware the evaluator effect: one evaluator/team finds a non-overlapping subset of issues.** In CUE studies 60–75% of issues were found by only one team. Add a second evaluator; severity ratings have poor inter-rater agreement, so use a fixed scale, multiple independent raters, and concrete examples.
- **Reliability in qualitative data comes from repetition across people, not sample size.** One voice is not a pattern; two is a possible pattern; five distinct voices is a valid pattern even if the concept feels foreign. Never justify a change on a single quote. Cognitive/behavioral patterns repeat over small samples; opinions require huge samples and rarely repeat.
- **Test the whole integrated product at the real moment of use.** Users make no distinction between UI, help, and docs; testing components in isolation is futile. Highest-validity data catches people mid-task doing something for their own reasons (Time-Aware Research); studying users on their own machines reveals the technological ecosystem (tabs, bookmarks, comparison sites) invisible in a lab.
- **Match the statistical method to data type and design.** Decide: continuous (time, rating) vs. binary (pass/fail, conversion); estimating vs. comparing; benchmark vs. group comparison; within- vs. between-subjects. These forks pick the test. Behind every quantitative measure are qualitative judgments — all data carries human bias, and significance never says something is "right," only that something interesting is happening.
- **Name and account for bias; you can't eliminate it.** Every study has design, sampling, interviewer, sponsor, and social-desirability bias plus the Hawthorne effect. Collect satisfaction ratings anonymously and out of the moderator's view. Simply noting bias lets you weight results.
- **Eye tracking is a supplement, not a standalone, and its measures are comparative only.** Gaze ≈ attention (eye-mind hypothesis) but only captures foveal (~2°) vision — absence of fixation ≠ absence of attention. It can't answer comprehension, memory, or acceptance; combine with observation + verbal data. Use it only for actionable insight, follow parsimony (a magnifying glass often beats a microscope), and analyze numerical data, never eyeball heatmaps.
## Process, communication & ethics

- **Research is a leadership activity — involve the whole team throughout.** People who help gather insights look for chances to apply them; a circulated report changes nothing. The highest-impact, lowest-effort move is getting colleagues into the field/observation room — a single session can revise a team's belief structure and convert skeptics. Seeing is believing, and far stronger live than via recordings.
- **Findings accomplish nothing unless communicated as a story, delivered in the form each audience can act on.** Use highlight clips, quotes, photos, and sketches so users feel real and stakeholders invest. Translate "easy to clean" into actionable specs ("≤1mm join gap"). Bake analytics inline beside the observed problem and the metric you expect the fix to move — never in a separate section.
- **Fix the worst problems first; do the least you can do.** Prioritize by criticality = severity + frequency; commit to *something* for each top problem before moving on. Ask "what's the smallest change that stops the problem we *observed*?" Tweaks ship sooner, actually happen, and don't break working parts. When fixing, consider taking something away before adding — the real problem is often noise already on the page.
- **Defer to the participant; build rapport; listen far beyond "don't interrupt."** Interviewing is a learned skill, not conversation — speak ~20% of the time, use deliberate silence ("people speak in paragraphs"), mirror the participant's terminology (never correct it), assume they make sense, and capture the "doorknob" close. Take the blame in copy; never imply the user is at fault. Record everything; notes descriptive, not interpretive.
- **Experimentation now carries research ethics.** Informed consent, confidentiality (scrub PII), right to withdraw, debriefs, no deception; participants should face no risk beyond normal daily life. Manipulating socially-relevant information (Facebook mood study, OKCupid) crosses from feature testing into harm. Consent also builds the trust required to get natural behavior.
- **Be "data aware," not just "data driven" — instrumentation is itself a design problem.** Data-driven = data decides (only safe once question, goal, and measurement quality are nailed); data-informed = one input; data-aware = recognizing many data types answer many questions and that how you collect data is a creative design choice. Validate "big data" with "thick data."
- **The brand experience IS the user experience here.** For interactive products the interface visual design is the brand identity and its voice the brand personality; assess the name/logo in every real context (especially the small app-icon size).
